# The conversation itself

Getting the meeting is `outreach.md`. This is what to do once you're in it.

Most founders do the reaching part adequately and then waste the call by pitching. A badly run
interview is worse than no interview: it returns false positives you then build on.

---

## The three rules (Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*)

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Not "do you like this?" but "how do you handle this
   today?"
2. **Ask about specific past events, not future hypotheticals.** "Would you buy a product that
   does X?" asks an optimistic person for an opinion about a situation that doesn't exist. "Walk
   me through the last time this happened" asks for a fact they cannot politely distort.
3. **Talk less.** They should be speaking ~80% of the time.

The failure mode the book is named for: your mom will say your idea is great. So will most
strangers, because agreeing is cheaper than disagreeing. Design questions that a polite person
cannot answer flatteringly.

## Do not mention your idea in the first 15 minutes

Once they know what you're building, every subsequent answer is shaded to be encouraging. Get the
factual account of their current process first. Introduce the idea at the end, if at all, and only
as the rung-4 ask.

## Question bank

Open with the process, not the pain — people describe workflows accurately and describe feelings
aspirationally.

**Their current process**
- Walk me through the last time you had to do <task>. Start from what triggered it.
- Who else touches it? What do they do with it?
- What tools are open on your screen while you do this?
- How long did that take, start to finish?
- How often does that happen — weekly, monthly, per deal?

**Finding the real pain (listen for irritation, not answers)**
- Which part of that do you dread?
- What breaks most often? What did you do the last time it broke?
- What happens downstream if it's wrong?
- Has anyone complained about it? Who, and to whom?

**Pricing the pain — this is what upgrades a complaint to an opportunity**
- Who does this work, and roughly what does that role cost you?
- Have you paid anyone outside the company to help with it? What did that cost?
- Have you tried to fix it before? What happened?
- Is there a line item in a budget for this today?

**Sizing the alternatives**
- What did you evaluate before settling on <current tool>? Why did you pick it?
- What would have to be true for you to switch?
- What's the workaround you'd be embarrassed to show me? *(then ask to see it — that's rung 3)*

**Authority**
- If you wanted a $200/month tool for this, who signs off?
- Have you bought anything like that recently? What was that process like?

**Closing**
- Who else should I be talking to about this? *(the single highest-ROI question in the set)*
- Can I come back to you when I've talked to ten more people?

## Never ask

- "Would you use this?" / "Would you pay for this?" — free yes.
- "How much would you pay?" — they invent a number with no consequences attached.
- "Do you think this is a good idea?" — you are asking them to be your friend.
- "Would this be useful?" — everything is useful in the abstract.
- Any question containing the words "would you" about the future.

## Customer Problem Stack Ranking

A sharper alternative to interviewing when you want to know not *whether* your problem is real but
*where it ranks* against everything else your buyer is dealing with. Attributed to Shreyas Doshi
and used at Stripe; written up in detail in r/startups (see `sources.md`). The author reported
learning more in two hours of stack ranking than in 100+ discovery interviews — his team's
seven-months-of-research value proposition ranked **dead last** among the problems his target
users actually had.

How it runs:

1. **Write a broad question about the activity, not your problem.** "What is the most frustrating
   part of <the activity your product sits inside>?" Broad enough that people surface problems you
   didn't think of.
2. **Convert your idea into a problem statement.** Not "an app that splits group holiday costs"
   but "working out who owes what after a group trip". Asking people to rate your *idea* invites
   flattery; asking them to rate a *problem* against other problems does not.
3. **Collect the field.** Gather the problems people name in response to the open question, plus
   your problem statement, phrased in the same voice so yours doesn't stand out.
4. **Have a fresh set of target customers rank them** by how frustrating each is.
5. **Read the position, not the score.** A problem that ranks 7th out of 8 is a mild inconvenience
   no matter how many people agree it's annoying. You are looking for top-two.

Use it when: you have one idea and want a brutal, fast read on whether it matters. It pairs well
with Track B in `track-b-fast-iteration.md`. It does not replace interviews — ranking tells you
*what* matters, interviews tell you *why* and *what it costs*.

## Offer a trade

The reliable way to get people to give you time in public communities, and the mechanic behind one
of the better-documented validation write-ups on r/microsaas: post asking your target users a few
questions, and **offer something concrete in return** — feedback on what they're building, the
aggregated results, a teardown, an hour of your own expertise. That founder got 8-10 conversations
from two Reddit posts this way and is now around $7.3k/mo.

Reciprocity beats persuasion. "Answer three questions and I'll send you what the other twenty
people said" costs you nothing and converts far better than asking for a favour.

Then actually send it. That follow-through is what turns a research list into your Phase 6
distribution channel.

## What to record

Immediately after, while it's fresh — five minutes, not later:

- Their exact words for the problem. **Use their vocabulary in your landing page headline**, not
  yours. This is half the value of doing interviews at all.
- The workaround, in concrete steps.
- Numbers: hours, frequency, headcount, spend.
- Highest rung reached (`outreach.md` § commitment ladder).
- Who they referred you to.
- What surprised you. If nothing surprised you, you were probably talking too much.

## How to know the interview went badly

- You spoke more than they did.
- They said "that sounds useful" and you wrote it down as a positive signal.
- You got compliments instead of facts.
- You left without a number, a workaround, or a name.
- You described your product in the first ten minutes.

## How many

Five conversations tell you whether you're in the right neighbourhood. Fifteen to twenty in the
same role start repeating, and the repetition is the signal — when you can predict the answer
before they give it, you understand the segment. Stop when new interviews stop surprising you,
then go get the rung-4 commitments.
