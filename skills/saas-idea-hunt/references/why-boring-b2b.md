# Category selection

## Bias toward

Boring, regulated, unglamorous, and operationally messy. The more painful the vertical is to learn,
the fewer people compete, and the higher the willingness to pay — a practitioner in the source
thread runs multiple products in healthcare compliance, infrastructure, and insurance and reports
the same pattern: nobody wants to build claims processing or HIPAA-compliant monitoring, which is
exactly why the margins hold.

Markers of a good category:
- The buyer has a P&L line for the problem already (software, headcount, or an agency).
- Regulation or audit requirements make errors expensive.
- Two systems that must agree and don't.
- Incumbent software is 15+ years old, sold by a rep, priced by quote.
- The industry has a trade association, a conference, and a forum — a reachable buyer population.
- You can name the job title of the person who suffers.

Starting list: field service, claims processing, compliance reporting, freight and drayage,
dental/veterinary practice management, K-12 and district admin, construction takeoff and lien
waivers, insurance brokerage, municipal permitting, lab/LIMS, trucking ELD/IFTA, franchise ops,
title and escrow, medical billing, waste hauling, equipment rental, staffing agency back office.

## Avoid

**Developer tools.** Developers expect free, evaluate on principle, and will build their own
version before paying. The source author spent two months on one and got four paying customers.
Exception: tools where the buyer is a platform team with a budget and the pain is compliance or
cost, not convenience.

**Consumer apps.** Weeks-scale churn and no budget line. B2B tools become part of a workflow and
stick for years.

**Products whose buyer is another founder.** Small market, no budget, high churn, and everyone in
it is also trying to sell you something. The source thread is itself an example — it reads to much
of its own audience as an ad for an idea-mining tool.

**Anything you picked because it was interesting.** Interest is not a market signal. If you cannot
find 40 complaint rows in 90 minutes, the category is not the problem — the absence of evidence is
the answer.

## The scale caveat

One commenter raised it and it is fair: B2B verticals have ceilings. A niche with 4,000 addressable
companies at $200/month caps around $9.6M ARR at full penetration, which nobody reaches. Decide up
front which you want:

- **$10-50k MRR lifestyle business:** a narrow vertical is ideal. Ceiling is a feature.
- **Venture-scale:** the wedge is still a narrow vertical complaint, but you need a credible path
  from it to adjacent verticals or up-market. Write that path down in the opportunity brief, or
  admit you are building the first thing.

Both are legitimate. Choosing accidentally is not.
