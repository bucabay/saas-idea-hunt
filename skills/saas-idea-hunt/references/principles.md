# Principles

The doctrine behind the phases, distilled from the source thread. Quotes are from the post and its
commenters. When a phase decision is ambiguous, decide by these.

---

## Validation

**Frustrated paying customers are the only validated demand.**
> "filter by 1-2 star reviews... frustrated paying customers = validated demand"

Someone already paying for a bad solution has a budget, an approver, and a workflow. Someone
agreeing that your idea sounds good has none of those. Weight every signal by whether money is
already moving.

**A manual hire is a six-figure spec sheet.**
> "If a 50-person company is hiring a 'compliance data coordinator'... that's a six-figure SaaS
> waiting to be built."

A job posting for glue work is the strongest single signal in this playbook: the pain is
articulated, quantified, budgeted, and approved, and the hiring manager is a warm contact. Mine job
boards before you mine anything else if you are short on time. See
`mining-playbook.md` § Labor signals.

**Firsthand experience compounds.**
> "firsthand experience gives you an enormous advantage"

If you have worked in the vertical, your judgement about which complaints are load-bearing is worth
more than fifty reviews, and you can skip weeks of Track A. If you have not, deep customer
conversations are the substitute — not more reading. Two hours with a practitioner beats two days
of review mining. Prefer categories where you have experience or direct access; if you have
neither, budget for the learning curve explicitly rather than pretending it away.

**Charge from day one.**
> "Paying users = real demand. If no one has paid yet then it's not validated."

Free trials without a card measure curiosity. Take payment details at signup — paid pilot,
time-limited trial with a card on file, or a straight price. This is the same claim as the
commitment ladder in `outreach.md`: rungs 1-3 are information, rung 5 is validation.

The caveat: charging early raises the bar on onboarding. A paying user who cannot reach first value
in one session churns immediately and takes the signal with them. Ship the price *and* the shortest
possible path to the outcome, or you will conclude the market said no when your onboarding did.

## Product

**Polish helps; it does not substitute for research.**
> "A rough product can still get traction if it solves a painful enough ops problem"

The source author's first successful MVP was ugly and barely functional: 14 signups in week one,
9 converted. Design effort spent before the pain is confirmed is the most expensive kind of
procrastination. After confirmation, polish is real leverage — but the ordering is not negotiable.

**Retention before acquisition.**
> "Retention > acquisition. It's up to 25x cheaper to retain than acquire."
> "That's not a growth problem — it's a product problem wearing a growth costume."

Pouring acquisition into a leaky bucket teaches you nothing and costs money. Before scaling any
channel, know your monthly logo churn and why each churned account left. At early stage optimise
for **learning velocity**, not growth rate: talk to every user, understand every signup, understand
every churn. This is why Phase 7 exists and why it gates Phase 6 scale-up.

**Avoid what clones in weeks.**
> "Mid-Low efforts SaaS that can be replicated within weeks wont last long imo"

If the whole product is a thin wrapper, a prompt, or one API call, the wedge is not a business —
it is a feature someone ships next month. Defensibility comes from domain depth, regulatory
burden, proprietary data, integrations that took real work, or distribution you own. Scoring factor
7 in `scoring.md` is where this gets an honest number.

## Market and risk

**The gaps close fast.**
> "it feels like every 'gap' gets filled within weeks of someone spotting it."

Publicly visible, easily-searched gaps are being mined by everyone reading the same threads. Two
defences: go where the search is hard (private communities, job boards, offline verticals,
regulated niches nobody wants to learn), and move on validation while the ledger is warm. A
complaint cluster you sit on for three months is someone else's launched product.

**AI compresses the build, not the understanding.**
> "AI can compress the build cycle, but it doesn't compress the time needed to understand a market."

Cheap building means more competitors reaching the same feature parity, faster. The scarce input is
market knowledge and buyer access. Spend the time AI gave you back on customers, not on more
features.

**Distribution is the moat.**
> "distribution is the moat"

When anyone can build it, the winner is whoever owns the channel to the buyer. Choose opportunities
where you can assemble a buyer list — review authors, job posters, an association directory, a
community you are already credible in. Scoring factor 6 (buyer reachability) is doing more work
than it looks like. One channel, thirty days, compounding — six channels at once is how you learn
nothing about any of them.

## Scale honesty

> "Problem with B2B is scale"

A narrow vertical caps out. Decide up front whether you are building a $10-50k MRR business, where
the ceiling is a feature, or something venture-scale, where the vertical is a wedge and you owe
yourself a written path out of it. Both are fine. Discovering which one you built, two years in, is
not. See `why-boring-b2b.md` § The scale caveat.
